Chapter 20Part IV · How the payroll engine works

Categories: how rules talk to each other

Running totals that rules add to and later rules read: the gross, the bases, the net

After this chapter you can
  • Define a salary category as a running total, and say why rules read totals rather than each other
  • Walk the standard chain BASIC → ALW → GROSS → DED → NET, with employer contributions outside the net
  • Explain how one rule feeds several totals, with Marie's company car
  • Say who reads categories after the payslip is computed
  • Check that a country's categories match its legal bases

Belgium adds a new tax-free allowance. The social-security rule, the withholding rule and the net rule do not change: the new rule simply joins the right totals, and every rule that reads those totals sees it. That is the whole point of categories: rules never name each other; they meet in shared totals.

What a salary category is

A salary category (salary rule category in Odoo) is a named running total. Each rule adds its result to its categories; later rules read the total instead of naming every rule that fed it. Categories are how rules talk to each other.

Three things follow:

  • A new rule needs no change elsewhere. Put a new allowance in the allowances total, and the gross and the net pick it up.
  • One rule can feed several totals. A contribution can lower both the net and the taxable base.
  • A total is not a line. It lives during the computation. When the law needs it on the payslip, a rule copies it into a line (that is what the GROSS rule does).

Illustrative numbers. The net rule names three totals, not five rules.

The standard chain

The payroll framework ships seven categories, and every country starts from them. Five form a chain from the basic salary to the net:

BASICpay for the time worked and paidcomes first: everything else derives from it
→
ALWallowances: everything paid on top of basicearnings that are not time
→
GROSS"Taxable Salary": basic + allowances so fara snapshot taken before taxes are computed on it
→
DEDdeductions: contributions, tax, garnishmentseverything that lowers the net
→
NETbasic + allowances + deductionswhat the bank transfer pays
COMP (company contribution): the employer's contributions. Recorded, booked and declared, but outside the chain: the net formula never reads it, because the employee does not pay them.
EXTRA_HOURS: overtime pay, kept apart so a country can treat it differently (for example, exclude it from the hours that set the hourly rate).

Two words decide where a rule lands: its category and its position.

Key idea: category and position

  • GROSS is "taxable" by position. It reads BASIC + ALW at the moment it runs. An expense reimbursement is in ALW too, but runs after GROSS: the net includes it, the taxable salary does not. An expense paid back is not salary.
  • Garnishments come after taxes because the law protects a part of the net (Chapter 6); the seizable amount is known only once taxes are withheld.
  • Employer contributions stay out of the net by category, not by position. The net reads BASIC, ALW and DED, never COMP, wherever the employer rules run.

Where the worked days go

Before any rule runs, each worked-days line adds its amount to the categories of its time type (Chapter 14). Attendance usually feeds BASIC; a paid leave type feeds what the country decides; unpaid leave is worth 0 and adds nothing. That is how time reaches the totals: the basic salary rule often just reads categories['BASIC'].

Common pitfall: a paid time type with no category

If a paid time type has no category, its amount is on the worked-days line but never reaches BASIC, so the gross and the net miss it. When a country adds a time type, check its categories. The opposite mistake also exists: a rule that both reads a total seeded by worked days and writes into it counts the amount twice.

One rule, several totals: Marie's company car

Belgian law needs three bases, not one: the running net (what will be paid), the contribution base (what social security is computed on) and the taxable base. So Belgium keeps one total per base, and each rule says which bases it belongs to.

Marie's company car is a benefit in kind: taxed as if it were salary, but never paid in cash (Chapter 2). Its rule, ATN.CAR, adds 136.77 € to the taxable base and nothing to the running net. The social-security rule adds its −980.25 € to both.

Marie, February 2026 Line total Running net Taxable base
Worked days (attendance) 7,500.00 7,500.00
ONSS social security, 13.07 % −980.25 6,519.75 6,519.75
ATN.CAR company car 136.77 6,519.75 6,656.52
GROSS reads the taxable base 6,656.52

The car changes the tax, not the cash: 7,500.00 − 980.25 + 136.77 = 6,656.52 € of taxable salary. The withholding rule, the net rule and the declarations never mention the car; they read totals. A new benefit in kind next year will be one more rule in the taxable base.

Worked example: Mélanie's three bases

Mélanie has no benefit in kind. Her social security (−522.80 €) lowers the running net and the taxable base but not the contribution base, which stays at 4,000.00 €. So her GROSS line, the taxable salary, is 3,477.20 €, not 4,000.00 €. After withholding (−826.69 €) and a special contribution (−36.24 €), the net rule reads the running net: 2,614.27 €.

Parent categories roll up

A category can have a parent. When a rule adds to a category, the engine also adds to its parent, and to the parent's parent. A country can then make the generic chain more precise without rewriting it.

ALW · allowancesread by the net rule
TAXABLE_ALWcommissions, bonuses: read by GROSS
exempt allowancesadded to ALW directly: in the net, not taxed
DED · deductionsread by the net rule
IMSS_EMPLOYEEemployee social security
income tax (ISR)added to DED directly

Mexico, as shipped. Its two formulas:

# GROSS, the taxable salary: only the taxable allowances
result = categories['BASIC'] + categories['TAXABLE_ALW']
# NET: unchanged from the standard; the children roll up into ALW and DED
result = categories['BASIC'] + categories['ALW'] + categories['DED']

The net formula is the generic one, yet it sees commissions and employee social security, because their categories roll up into ALW and DED. The taxable salary reads only the child, so exempt allowances stay out of it.

Who reads categories after the payslip

Categories exist during the computation; afterwards, each line remembers its rule, and the rule its categories. That is enough for everything downstream:

Who Reads
Declarations and yearly certificates the sum of a category over a period, across validated payslips: the taxable base of the quarter, the tax withheld in the year (Chapter 17)
Payroll reports payslip lines grouped by rule or by category
The journal entry each rule's accounts, not its categories (Chapter 16)
The payslip's gross and net fields the lines with codes BASIC, GROSS, NET

In practice

If a declaration total is wrong while every payslip looks right, look for a rule in the wrong category. A declaration sums categories, so designing a country's categories is designing its declarations: start from the boxes of the forms.

How countries extend the chain

The rule of thumb: one category per legal base. Whatever amount the law computes something on (a contribution base, a taxable base, a pensionable salary) gets its own total, and each rule says which bases it belongs to. Countries keep the codes BASIC, GROSS and NET, because the payslip fields and reports read them, and add what their law needs between them.

Around the world

  • Mexico keeps the generic tree, adds child categories (TAXABLE_ALW, IMSS_EMPLOYEE) and a separate total for the social-security base, SBC, capped by its own rule.
  • India keeps the generic formulas: GROSS = BASIC + ALW, so there GROSS really is the gross salary.
  • Belgium uses a running net: one total (REMUNERATION_BASE) that every rule paying or withholding cash feeds, plus a contribution base and a taxable base. The net rule just reads the running net.
  • United States keeps pre-tax deductions in their own category, because they lower the taxable wages.

Common pitfall: GROSS does not mean the same everywhere

The GROSS category is labelled Taxable Salary. In India it is the gross; in Mexico, the taxable part of the earnings; in Belgium, the salary after employee social security (Mélanie: 3,477.20 €, not 4,000.00 €). Before comparing "gross" across countries, or building a report on it, open the GROSS rule and read its formula.

Review checklist

  • Every legal base has a category: list the bases in the law (contribution base, taxable base, pensionable pay…) and find each one.
  • Each rule feeds exactly the bases the law says: for every earning, ask "is it subject to contributions? to tax? paid in cash?" and compare with its categories.
  • Is GROSS gross or taxable here? Read the formula, and check the reports and declarations that use it agree.
  • Every paid time type has a category, and no rule both reads and writes the same seeded total.
  • Employer contributions are outside the net by category, whatever their position.

Summary

  • A category is a named running total: rules add to it, later rules read it, and rules never name each other.
  • The standard chain is BASIC → ALW → GROSS (taxable salary) → DED → NET; employer contributions (COMP) stay outside the net.
  • Category decides which totals a rule changes; position decides which rules see it. A reimbursement after GROSS is paid but not taxed.
  • Worked days add their amounts to their time types' categories before the first rule.
  • One rule can feed several totals: Marie's car is in the taxable base, not in the running net.
  • Parent categories roll up, so a country can refine the chain without changing the net formula.
  • Declarations sum categories over a period; one category per legal base is the design rule.
